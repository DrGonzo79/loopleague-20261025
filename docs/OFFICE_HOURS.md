# Office Hours: product diagnostic

## Specific customer

The economic buyer is a mid-market footwear, beverage, or snack brand manager who already funds youth activations. The practical channel customer is the brand's agency operator. Faculty sponsors and student captains are users, not payers.

## Painful status quo

An emerging school trend has energy but no verified roster, consistent weekly challenge, rights-cleared media, or cross-school result. An agency must assemble these manually before it can promise a sponsor-safe activation. The pain is episodic and modest; the report's 2/10 rating is a warning against generic SaaS.

## Desperate use case

An agency has a signed or near-signed youth brief and eight weeks to prove a new activation format across 20 schools. It needs one operator to recruit faculty sponsors, enforce consent gates, normalize submissions, publish results, and deliver a recap—not a platform subscription.

## Narrowest paid wedge

Sell a fixed-scope, $10K-$15K managed regional season. LoopLeague is internal operator tooling. No inventory, public voting, open student accounts, or AI moderation until the service has repeated twice.

## Observation and validation

1. Interview five youth-sports activation agency leads and ask them to rank the operational blockers from a real brief.
2. Put a one-page scope and $12,500 price in front of eight warm brand/agency contacts.
3. Count explicit offers to introduce the operator to a budget owner and explicit willingness to fund a pilot.
4. Shadow one school approval workflow; time faculty confirmation and consent completion.
5. Kill the thesis if there are fewer than three qualified calls or zero budget-positive responses after eight warm introductions.

## Future fit

Only after two paid seasons should the repeated workflow become software: permission ledger, submission QA, sponsor reporting, and reusable school relationships. The durable asset would be verified distribution and rights history—not a leaderboard UI.
